Nikon Z 180-600mm for bird photography: the numbers, worked out

What the NIKKOR Z 180-600mm f/5.6-6.3 VR puts on a bird at 600 mm, worked out from the focal length and the sensors of five popular bodies: pixels across birds from chickadee to heron at 10, 25 and 50 m, what the Z Teleconverter TC-1.4x and TC-2.0x add, how far you can crop and still meet iNaturalist and contest sizes, and how much the wingtips blur in flight.

Short answer: at 600 mm the Z 180-600 puts about 1,357 pixels across an American robin at 25 m on the Nikon Z8 / Z9 (full-frame) and 1,398 on the Nikon Z50II (APS-C). On the Nikon Z8 / Z9, crop to iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels and the robin still fills 66% of the width. Crop from the full-size original, and never upscale a crop to reach a size.

“Won't fit” means the bird is longer than the frame is wide at that distance. The sum: the bird's length × focal length ÷ (distance − focal length) gives its size on the sensor, divided by the pixel pitch (Nikon Z50II 4.2 µm, Nikon Z6III / Z5II 5.9 µm, Nikon Z8 / Z9 4.3 µm).

What the camera body changes

The lens puts the same image of the bird on every sensor: at 600 mm, an American robin at 25 m is 5.90 mm long on the sensor. The body decides how many pixels cover that image. In pixels per millimetre of sensor: Nikon Z50II 237, Nikon Z6III / Z5II 168, Nikon Z8 / Z9 230. So on the same lens, at the same distance, the Nikon Z50II puts 1.41 times as many pixels on the bird as the Nikon Z6III / Z5II: 1,398 against 994 across that robin. The APS-C Nikon Z50II and the full-frame Nikon Z8 / Z9 have pixels of almost the same size (4.22 and 4.35 µm), so they put almost the same pixels on the bird (1,398 and 1,357 across that robin); the Nikon Z8 / Z9 records a wider frame around it. A smaller sensor adds pixels on the bird only when its pixels are smaller (crop sensor vs full frame).

What a teleconverter adds

Nikon lists its Z Teleconverter TC-1.4x and TC-2.0x for this lens. A teleconverter multiplies the focal length: 600 mm becomes 840 mm with the 1.4x and 1,200 mm with the 2x. On the Nikon Z8 / Z9, that takes an American robin at 25 m from 1,357 pixels to 1,919 or 2,783:

Crop tighter and the file drops below the size; enlarging it to reach the number adds no detail, and the contests that ask for full-size files say so (crop rules and minimum sizes for 11 contests). Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates (est.), so treat these as close guides rather than exact figures. Holding the osprey's wingtips within 3 pixels at this distance would take about 1/16000; for the bald eagle, about 1/12800. The wings slow to a stop at the top and bottom of each stroke, which is why a burst catches sharp wingtips that a single frame usually misses.

Questions

Is the Z 180-600 long enough for bird photography?

It depends on the bird and the distance. At 600 mm it puts about 1,357 pixels across an American robin at 25 m on the Nikon Z8 / Z9 and 670 at 50 m; a black-capped chickadee at 25 m gets 763. On the Nikon Z50II the same robin at 25 m gets 1,398. Crop the Nikon Z8 / Z9 frame to iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels and the robin still fills 66% of the width.

Does the Z 180-600 take a teleconverter?

Yes. Nikon lists its Z Teleconverter TC-1.4x and TC-2.0x for this lens. They take 600 mm to 840 mm and 1,200 mm. On the Nikon Z8 / Z9, an American robin at 25 m goes from 1,357 pixels at 600 mm to 1,919 with the 1.4x and 2,783 with the 2x.

Does an APS-C body give the Z 180-600 more reach?

Only when its pixels are smaller. In pixels per millimetre of sensor: Nikon Z50II 237, Nikon Z6III / Z5II 168, Nikon Z8 / Z9 230. On the same lens, at the same distance, the Nikon Z50II puts 1.41 times as many pixels on the bird as the Nikon Z6III / Z5II. An APS-C sensor doesn't change the focal length; it records a smaller frame around the same image of the bird. The APS-C Nikon Z50II and the full-frame Nikon Z8 / Z9 have pixels of almost the same size (4.22 and 4.35 µm), so they put almost the same pixels on the bird (1,398 and 1,357 across that robin); the Nikon Z8 / Z9 records a wider frame around it.

What shutter speed freezes a bird in flight at 600 mm?

On the Nikon Z8 / Z9, with an osprey at about 43 m, wings across two thirds of the frame, the wingtips blur about 24 pixels at 1/2000 and 12 at 1/4000 mid-stroke when you pan with the bird. Holding them within 3 pixels would take about 1/16000, so a burst is what catches the wings sharp near the top or bottom of the stroke. Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates.
