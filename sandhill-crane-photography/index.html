Sandhill Crane photography: shutter speed, lens and crop for sandhill cranes in flight

The numbers for photographing a Sandhill Crane in flight, worked out from published wingspan, flight-speed and wingbeat figures: the shutter speed that freezes the wingtips and the body, how many pixels the spread wings fill with 400, 600 and 800 mm lenses at 25, 50 and 100 m, and how far away the bird can be for a 2,048-pixel image or an A4 print.

Short answer: pan with the crane and its body stays put in the frame, so the wingtips set the shutter speed. With its wings across two thirds of the frame on a Canon EOS R7 and 600 mm lens (about 83 m away), 1/2000 leaves the wingtips about 19 px of blur mid-stroke, and keeping them sharp on screen (3 px) takes about 1/16000, beyond the 1/8000 top speed of most mechanical shutters. A burst catches them: at 1/2000, about 1 in 10 frames have the wingtips within 3 px, about 2.0 frames a second at 20 fps. Held still instead, the camera needs about 1/12800 to keep its body within 3 px as it flies across. At 50 m with 600 mm, its wings span 4,060 px on a 24-megapixel full-frame body and are wider than the frame on the 32.5-megapixel R7.

Shutter speed for sandhill cranes in flight

From our shutter-speed calculator with its default setup: a Canon EOS R7 (32.5 megapixels) and a 600 mm lens. You pan with the bird, so its body stays put in the frame and the blur that's left comes from the wings, each taken to sweep a 100° stroke at the bird's wingbeat rate, fastest mid-stroke. Blur is counted in the photo's own pixels at 100%: up to 3 pixels is sharp on screen, 1 pixel is pixel-sharp. The camera-still column is for a bird crossing in front of a camera that doesn't move, at its cruising speed.

Its wingbeat rate and cruising speed are estimates (sources below), so treat these as close guides rather than exact figures. Holding the wingtips pixel-sharp (1 px) with its wings across two thirds of the frame would take about 1/39,000. The wings stop for an instant at the top and bottom of each stroke, which is why a burst catches sharp wingtips that a single frame usually misses. Stepping back until the wings span a third of the frame halves the blur; crop in afterwards.

How far can you crop?

Most size rules count pixels on the long edge. An image 2,048 pixels wide can come from as little as 35% of the R6 Mark II's 6,000-pixel width, 25% of the Z8's 8,256-pixel width and 30% of the R7's 6,960-pixel width. A landscape A4 print at 300 ppi (29.7 × 21 cm) needs 3,508 × 2,480 pixels: 59% of the width and 62% of the height on the R6 Mark II, 43% of the width and 46% of the height on the Z8 and 51% of the width and 54% of the height on the R7.

These distances count pixels only; at long range, heat haze and focus cost detail too.
