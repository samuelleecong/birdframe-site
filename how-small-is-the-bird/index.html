How small is the bird?

Bird photographers say they never have enough lens. I wanted a number for it, so I ran BirdFrame over 1,995 public-domain bird photos from iNaturalist and measured every bird it found: 4,511 in all.

The typical bird fills 6.6% of the frame

For each photo I took the main bird, the largest one found in it, and measured how much of the frame it covers. In half the photos it's under 6.6%. A quarter are under 1.9%, and only a quarter are over 17%.

Why the full-size original matters

Shrink a 24-megapixel photo to 2048 pixels wide, a common size for sharing, and you keep 12% of its pixels. A bird that fills 5% of the frame goes from 1.2 megapixels to 0.14. At 1% of the frame, it goes from 490 pixels square to 167.

More than one bird

BirdFrame found more than one bird in 30% of the photos, and 49 in the busiest frame. These counts are rougher than the sizes above (see the method), but they're a reminder that one photo can hold several birds worth cropping.

Method

Photos. The 2,000 most recent research-grade bird observations on iNaturalist with a public-domain (CC0) photo, one photo each: 1,995 unique photos, observed between 2013 and 2026, at iNaturalist's largest size (up to 2048 pixels on the long edge).

Birds. BirdFrame 1.2 on a Mac found 4,511 birds in 1,968 photos, and none in 27. A bird's size is the area of the box around it as a share of the whole photo. The main bird is the largest one in each photo.

Checks. In a hand check of 40 random finds, 35 were birds, 2 were unclear and 3 weren't birds (a reflection, a leaf and a streaked rock). In 10 random photos with three or more finds, 70 of 72 finds were birds, and birds in big flocks were undercounted. So the main-bird figures are solid, and per-bird counts are approximate.

Caveats. These are documentation photos, and many iNaturalist users crop before they upload, so the frames straight out of a camera probably hold even smaller birds. The sample leans to recent uploads, and species samples are small.
