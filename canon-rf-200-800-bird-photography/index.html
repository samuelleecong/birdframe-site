Canon RF 200-800mm for bird photography: the numbers, worked out

What the Canon RF200-800mm F6.3-9 IS USM puts on a bird at 800 mm, worked out from the focal length and the sensors of five popular bodies: pixels across birds from chickadee to heron at 10, 25 and 50 m, what the Extender RF1.4x and RF2x add, how far you can crop and still meet iNaturalist and contest sizes, and how much the wingtips blur in flight.

Short answer: at 800 mm the RF 200-800 puts about 1,326 pixels across an American robin at 25 m on the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8 (full-frame) and 2,476 on the Canon EOS R7 (APS-C). On the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8, crop to iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels and the robin still fills 65% of the width. Crop from the full-size original, and never upscale a crop to reach a size.

What the camera body changes

The lens puts the same image of the bird on every sensor: at 800 mm, an American robin at 25 m is 7.93 mm long on the sensor. The body decides how many pixels cover that image. In pixels per millimetre of sensor: Canon EOS R10 269, Canon EOS R7 312, Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8 167, Canon EOS R5 228. So on the same lens, at the same distance, the Canon EOS R7 puts 1.87 times as many pixels on the bird as the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8: 2,476 against 1,326 across that robin. A smaller sensor adds pixels on the bird only when its pixels are smaller (crop sensor vs full frame).

What an extender adds

Canon lists the Extender RF1.4x and Extender RF2x for this lens. An extender multiplies the focal length: 800 mm becomes 1,120 mm with the 1.4x and 1,600 mm with the 2x. On the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8, that takes an American robin at 25 m from 1,326 pixels to 1,881 or 2,743:

Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates (est.), so treat these as close guides rather than exact figures. Holding the osprey's wingtips within 3 pixels at this distance would take about 1/12800; for the bald eagle, about 1/10000. The wings slow to a stop at the top and bottom of each stroke, which is why a burst catches sharp wingtips that a single frame usually misses.

Questions

Is the RF 200-800 long enough for bird photography?

It depends on the bird and the distance. At 800 mm it puts about 1,326 pixels across an American robin at 25 m on the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8 and 652 at 50 m; a black-capped chickadee at 25 m gets 746. On the Canon EOS R7 the same robin at 25 m gets 2,476. Crop the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8 frame to iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels and the robin still fills 65% of the width.

Does the RF 200-800 take an extender (teleconverter)?

Yes. Canon lists the Extender RF1.4x and Extender RF2x for this lens. They take 800 mm to 1,120 mm and 1,600 mm. On the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8, an American robin at 25 m goes from 1,326 pixels at 800 mm to 1,881 with the 1.4x and 2,743 with the 2x.

Does an APS-C body give the RF 200-800 more reach?

Only when its pixels are smaller. In pixels per millimetre of sensor: Canon EOS R10 269, Canon EOS R7 312, Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8 167, Canon EOS R5 228. On the same lens, at the same distance, the Canon EOS R7 puts 1.87 times as many pixels on the bird as the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8. An APS-C sensor doesn't change the focal length; it records a smaller frame around the same image of the bird.

What shutter speed freezes a bird in flight at 800 mm?

On the Canon EOS R6 Mark II / R8, with an osprey at about 57 m, wings across two thirds of the frame, the wingtips blur about 17 pixels at 1/2000 and 9 at 1/4000 mid-stroke when you pan with the bird. Holding them within 3 pixels would take about 1/12800, so a burst is what catches the wings sharp near the top or bottom of the stroke. Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates.
