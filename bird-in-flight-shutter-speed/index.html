How it works

You pan with the bird, so its body stays put in the frame and the blur that's left comes from the flapping wings. Each wing is taken to sweep through an arc of 100° at the bird's wingbeat rate: fastest mid-stroke, and stopping for an instant at the top and bottom. The calculator turns that wingtip speed into pixels on your sensor, from the lens's magnification at your distance and the size of your camera's pixels, and measures blur at 100%. The camera-still line uses the bird's cruising speed instead, for a bird crossing in front of a camera that doesn't move. Real birds speed up, glide, turn and flap harder on take-off, so treat every number as an estimate.

Questions

What shutter speed do I need for birds in flight?

It depends on what you want sharp. For the body of a bird you're panning with, many bird photographers start around 1/2000. Freezing the wingtips mid-stroke can take far more: the tip of a wing 0.9 m long, beating 2.5 times a second through a 100° stroke, peaks at about 12 metres a second, and close up that can call for 1/8000 or faster. Set your camera, lens, distance and bird above to see the speed for your shot.

Why are the wingtips blurred when the eye is sharp?

Panning cancels the bird's flight, not its flapping. Mid-stroke, the wingtips sweep up and down past the body at several metres a second, while the head barely moves against your lens. So a frame can have a sharp eye and soft wingtips, and many photographers like a little wingtip blur because it shows motion.

Does a faster burst help?

Yes. Twice in every wingbeat, at the top and the bottom of the stroke, the wings stop for an instant as they change direction. Each frame is a chance to land near that moment, so more frames a second means more frames with sharp wings. The catch is finding them among hundreds of near-identical shots: the free Burst Picker finds the sharpest bird in each burst.

Why do distance and focal length matter?

Blur is counted in pixels, and the bigger the bird is in the frame, the more pixels each centimetre of wing movement crosses. Halve the distance or double the focal length and the blur roughly doubles at the same shutter speed, along with the bird's size. A camera with smaller pixels shows more blur in pixels for the same reason. APS-C crop mode on a full-frame camera doesn't change it: it only trims the frame.

What do 1 px and 3 px of blur mean?

They're how far a point on the bird smears across the photo while the shutter is open, counted in the photo's own pixels and seen at 100%, one photo pixel to one screen pixel. One pixel or less is pixel-sharp: as crisp as the sensor allows. Up to about 3 pixels is hard to see unless you zoom right in, and usually disappears once the photo is shrunk for a screen or the web. They're rules of thumb, not hard limits.
