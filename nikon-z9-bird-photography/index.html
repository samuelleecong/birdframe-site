Nikon Z9 for bird photography: the numbers, worked out

What the Nikon Z9 puts on a bird, worked out from its sensor and the lenses people pair with it: pixels across birds from chickadee to heron at 10, 25 and 50 m, how far you can crop and still meet iNaturalist and contest sizes, and how much the wingtips blur in flight.

Short answer: the Nikon Z9 records 8,256 × 5,504 pixels (45.4 megapixels) on a full-frame sensor of 35.9 × 23.9 mm, so each pixel is 4.3 µm across. With a 600 mm lens that puts about 1,357 pixels across an American robin at 25 m: crop to iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels and the robin still fills 66% of the width. Crop from the full-size original, and never upscale a crop to reach a size.

The Nikon Z9 has the same sensor size and pixel count as the Nikon Z8, so every figure here holds for the Z8 too (Z8 guide).

Does the DX crop mode add reach?

No. In DX crop mode the Z9 keeps the middle of the frame, about 5,504 × 3,669 pixels (20.2 megapixels) instead of 8,256 × 5,504, but the bird gets exactly the same pixels: 1,357 across an American robin at 25 m with 600 mm either way. The bird looks bigger in the viewfinder because the frame around it is smaller (25% of the width instead of 16%). Shooting the full frame and cropping later keeps every pixel of the bird and leaves room to straighten the horizon or include a wingtip you'd otherwise cut off.

How far you can crop and still meet real sizes

Nearly every size rule counts pixels on the long edge. Here is the smallest share of the Z9's 8,256-pixel width that a horizontal crop can keep for each, and how much of that crop an American robin at 25 m with 600 mm (1,357 px) would fill:

So with this robin you can crop until it fills 45% of the width and still have Audubon's 3,000 pixels. Crop tighter and the file drops below the size; enlarging it to reach the number adds no detail, and the contests that ask for full-size files say so (crop rules and minimum sizes for 11 contests). For the general case, see how much you can crop a bird photo. The rule that always holds: crop from the full-size original, never from a downsized export, and never upscale.

Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates (est.), so treat these as close guides rather than exact figures. Holding the osprey's wingtips within 3 pixels at this distance would take about 1/16000; for the bald eagle, about 1/12800. The wings slow to a stop at the top and bottom of each stroke, which is why a burst catches sharp wingtips that a single frame usually misses.

Questions

How many pixels does the Nikon Z9 put on a bird?

It depends on the lens and the distance. With 600 mm, about 1,357 pixels across an American robin at 25 m and 670 at 50 m; with 800 mm, 1,825 and 897. The sum: the bird's length × focal length ÷ (distance − focal length), divided by the pixel pitch (4.3 µm).

How far can you crop a Z9 photo for iNaturalist or a contest?

A horizontal crop has to keep 25% of the frame's width to fill iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels, 36% for Audubon's 3,000-pixel entries and 42% for Australian Geographic's 3,500-pixel finalist files (2026 rules). Crop from the full-size original and never enlarge a crop to reach a size.

Does the Z9's DX crop mode give more reach?

No. It keeps about 5,504 × 3,669 pixels from the middle of the frame instead of 8,256 × 5,504, but the bird gets the same pixels: 1,357 across an American robin at 25 m with a 600 mm lens either way. Shooting the full frame and cropping later keeps the same bird with more room around it.

What shutter speed freezes a bird in flight with the Z9?

With an osprey at about 43 m on 600 mm, wings across two thirds of the frame, the wingtips blur about 24 pixels at 1/2000 and 12 at 1/4000 mid-stroke when you pan with the bird. Holding them within 3 pixels would take about 1/16000, so a burst is what catches the wings sharp near the top or bottom of the stroke. Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates.
