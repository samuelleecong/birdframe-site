Is 400mm enough for bird photography?

Yes, for most birds, as long as you can get reasonably close. Taking “enough” to mean at least 1,500 pixels across the bird, enough for a sharp 8 × 10 inch print, a 400 mm lens covers a 14 cm songbird such as a European Robin out to about 8 to 9 m (25 to 29 ft) on a full-frame camera, and 10 to 13 m (34 to 42 ft) on APS-C or Micro Four Thirds. Bigger birds give you more room: a heron gets plenty at 30 m. Further away, a small bird shrinks to phone-screen or record-shot size, and 600 mm, 800 mm or getting closer is what helps.

Short answer: at 20 m (66 ft), 400 mm puts 558 to 940 pixels across a 14 cm songbird, depending on the camera: fine for a screen or a record shot, short of a big print. A 600 mm lens puts about 1.5 times as many pixels on the bird and 800 mm about twice as many, and halving the distance does the same as doubling the focal length.

What “enough” means here

Reach comes down to pixels on the bird: how many of the sensor's pixels the bird's image covers, which is what's left after you crop. This guide uses two yardsticks, both for a crop in which the bird spans half the picture's width:

  • 1,500 pixels across the bird gives a crop 3,000 pixels wide: an 8 × 10 inch print at 300 ppi (3,000 × 2,400 pixels).
  • 1,000 pixels across the bird gives a crop 2,000 pixels wide, more than a Full HD screen (1,920 pixels) shows.

Fewer pixels still work for a phone, social media or an eBird or iNaturalist record. The figures assume a sharp frame and a bird side-on. A soft frame, heat haze or a bird facing you all mean fewer useful pixels (how much you can crop for prints and screens).

Pixels across the bird = bird length × focal length ÷ (distance − focal length) × pixels across the sensor ÷ sensor width, with every length in millimetres. For a 14 cm European Robin at 20 m with 400 mm on a Canon EOS R7 (6,960 pixels across 22.3 mm): 140 × 400 ÷ 19,600 × 6,960 ÷ 22.3 = about 892 pixels.

When 400 mm is enough

  • Gardens, feeders, hides and known perches, where you can work within about 10 m (33 ft) of small birds.
  • Birds the size of a robin or bigger. A 24 cm American Robin at 20 m gets 956 pixels on a Sony A7 IV and 1,529 on a Canon EOS R7; herons, ducks and birds of prey get more.
  • Birds in flight, where a lighter lens and a wider view make the bird easier to find and follow.
  • APS-C and Micro Four Thirds cameras, which put about 1.4 to 1.7 times as many pixels on the bird as a 33 MP full-frame body with the same lens.

When it isn't

  • Small birds you can't approach, or across water or a mudflat. At 20 m a 14 cm bird is 558 to 940 pixels across, and at 30 m 369 to 622.
  • Big prints of small birds, unless you can get within 8 to 13 m.
  • 24-megapixel full-frame bodies, which pack fewer pixels into each millimetre: a Canon EOS R6 Mark II puts 478 pixels across the robin at 20 m.

Getting more from a 400 mm lens

  • Get closer. Halve the distance and you roughly double the pixels across the bird, with no light lost. A hide, a car or a quiet wait by a feeder often beats a longer lens.
  • Add a 1.4× teleconverter, if your lens takes one. It turns 400 mm into 560 mm, and the songbird at 20 m on the R7 from 892 pixels into 1,259, for one stop of light.

Questions

Is 400mm enough for birds in flight?

Usually. Birds in flight are often bigger or closer than a perched songbird, and a 400 mm lens is easier to swing and shows more sky around the bird. A 55 cm Red-tailed Hawk at 30 m gets 1,451 pixels on a 33 MP full-frame Sony A7 IV and 2,320 on a Canon EOS R7.

Is 400mm on Micro Four Thirds the same as 800mm?

For framing, roughly: 400 mm on an OM System OM-1 shows about the view of 800 mm on full frame. For pixels on the bird, not quite: the OM-1 puts 851 pixels across a 14 cm songbird at 20 m with 400 mm, where a Sony A7 IV with 800 mm puts 1,139. Its 20 megapixels sit on a sensor with about a quarter of the area.

Is a 100-400mm zoom enough for bird photography?

At 400 mm it puts as many pixels on the bird as any 400 mm lens, so these tables apply; how sharp they are depends on the lens. The shorter end helps with big or close birds, such as a heron, too long for the frame at 400 mm within about 11 m on full frame.

Should I get a 600mm lens instead?

If most of your birds are small and more than about 10 m away, yes: 600 mm puts about 1.5 times as many pixels across the bird. If you work from a hide or a garden, photograph birds in flight or bigger birds, or mostly share on screens, 400 mm and a careful crop is usually enough.
