Hummingbird photography: shutter speed, lens and crop for hummingbirds in flight

The numbers for photographing a Ruby-throated Hummingbird in flight, worked out from published wingspan, flight-speed and wingbeat figures: why no shutter speed freezes the wingtips, how many pixels the spread wings fill with 400, 600 and 800 mm lenses at 5, 10 and 20 m, and how far away the bird can be for a 2,048-pixel image or an A4 print.

Short answer: a hovering hummingbird's body barely moves, so the wingtips set the shutter speed, and no shutter speed freezes them. With its wings across two thirds of the frame on a Canon EOS R7 and 600 mm lens (about 4.7 m away), 1/2000 leaves the wingtips about 332 px of blur mid-stroke, and keeping them sharp on screen (3 px) would take about 1/220,000, beyond even the fastest camera shutters. A burst won't rescue it: the wings beat 53 times a second, so even a frame timed on the turn of the stroke blurs the wingtips about 7 px at 1/2000. That's why hummingbird photographers usually freeze the wings with flash: several flashes at low power, so the flash's duration, not the shutter, stops the motion (Audubon, Nikon). At 10 m with 600 mm, its wings span 1,067 px on a 24-megapixel full-frame body and 1,992 px on the 32.5-megapixel R7.

Shutter speed for hummingbirds in flight

From our shutter-speed calculator with its default setup: a Canon EOS R7 (32.5 megapixels) and a 600 mm lens. You pan with the bird (or it hovers), so its body stays put in the frame and the blur that's left comes from the wings, each taken to sweep a 100° stroke at the bird's wingbeat rate, fastest mid-stroke. Blur is counted in the photo's own pixels at 100%: up to 3 pixels is sharp on screen, 1 pixel is pixel-sharp. The camera-still column is for a bird crossing in front of a camera that doesn't move, at its cruising speed.

The camera-still column is for a hummingbird flying across, not hovering; its cruising speed is a rough estimate (sources below). Holding the wingtips pixel-sharp (1 px) with its wings across two thirds of the frame would take about 1/660,000. The wings stop for an instant at the top and bottom of each stroke, but at 53 wingbeats a second even that instant blurs at 1/2000, so no frame in a burst is within 3 px. Stepping back until the wings span a third of the frame halves the blur; crop in afterwards.

How far can you crop?

Most size rules count pixels on the long edge. An image 2,048 pixels wide can come from as little as 35% of the R6 Mark II's 6,000-pixel width, 25% of the Z8's 8,256-pixel width and 30% of the R7's 6,960-pixel width. A landscape A4 print at 300 ppi (29.7 × 21 cm) needs 3,508 × 2,480 pixels: 59% of the width and 62% of the height on the R6 Mark II, 43% of the width and 46% of the height on the Z8 and 51% of the width and 54% of the height on the R7.

How many pixels does a Ruby-throated Hummingbird fill with a 400, 600 or 800 mm lens?

Across its 0.10 m wingspan at 10 m, on a 24-megapixel full-frame body: 696 px with 400 mm, 1,067 px with 600 mm and 1,453 px with 800 mm. On the 32.5-megapixel Canon EOS R7: 1,300 px with 400 mm, 1,992 px with 600 mm and 2,714 px with 800 mm. Halve the distance and the pixels roughly double.

How far away can a Ruby-throated Hummingbird be for a 2,048-pixel image or an A4 print?

With the wings across half the crop's width, as the reach calculator frames it: with 600 mm on a 24-megapixel full-frame body, up to about 10 m for a 2,048-pixel-wide image and 5.9 m for an A4 print at 300 ppi; on the Canon EOS R7, 18 m and 10 m.
