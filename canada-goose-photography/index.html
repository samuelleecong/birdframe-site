Canada Goose photography: shutter speed, lens and crop for Canada geese in flight

The numbers for photographing a Canada Goose in flight, worked out from published wingspan, flight-speed and wingbeat figures: the shutter speed that freezes the wingtips and the body, how many pixels the spread wings fill with 400, 600 and 800 mm lenses at 25, 50 and 100 m, and how far away the bird can be for a 2,048-pixel image or an A4 print.

Short answer: pan with the Canada Goose and its body stays put in the frame, so the wingtips set the shutter speed. With its wings across two thirds of the frame on a Canon EOS R7 and 600 mm lens (about 62 m away), 1/2000 leaves the wingtips about 25 px of blur mid-stroke, and keeping them sharp on screen (3 px) takes about 1/20000, faster than the 1/8000 most shutters can do. A burst catches them: at 1/2000, about 1 in 13 frames have the wingtips within 3 px, about 1.5 a second at 20 frames a second. Held still instead, the camera needs about 1/20000 to keep a crossing Canada Goose's body within 3 px. At 50 m with 600 mm, the Canada Goose's wings span 3,025 px on a 24-megapixel full-frame body and 5,648 px on the 32.5-megapixel R7.

Shutter speed for Canada geese in flight

From our shutter-speed calculator with its default setup: a Canon EOS R7 (32.5 megapixels) and a 600 mm lens. You pan with the bird, so its body stays put in the frame and the blur that's left comes from the wings, each taken to sweep a 100° stroke at the bird's wingbeat rate, fastest mid-stroke. Blur is counted in the photo's own pixels at 100%: up to 3 pixels is sharp on screen, 1 pixel is pixel-sharp. The camera-still column is for a bird crossing in front of a camera that doesn't move, at its cruising speed.

The Canada Goose's wingbeat rate is an estimate (sources below), so treat these as close guides rather than exact figures. Holding the Canada Goose's wingtips pixel-sharp (1 px) with its wings across two thirds of the frame would take a shutter faster than 1/32000. The wings stop for an instant at the top and bottom of each stroke, which is why a burst catches sharp wingtips that a single frame usually misses. Stepping back until the wings span a third of the frame halves the blur, and you crop the rest later.

How far can you crop?

Most size rules count pixels on the long edge. An image 2,048 pixels wide can come from as little as 34% of the R6 Mark II's 6,000-pixel width, 25% of the Z8's 8,256-pixel width and 29% of the R7's 6,960-pixel width. A landscape A4 print at 300 ppi (29.7 × 21 cm) needs 3,508 × 2,480 pixels: 58% of the width and 62% of the height on the R6 Mark II, 42% of the width and 45% of the height on the Z8 and 50% of the width and 53% of the height on the R7.

How many pixels does a Canada Goose fill with a 400, 600 or 800 mm lens?

On the 32.5-megapixel Canon EOS R7: 3,750 px with 400 mm and 5,648 px with 600 mm; with 800 mm the wings are wider than the frame. Halve the distance and the pixels roughly double.

How far away can a Canada Goose be for a 2,048-pixel image or an A4 print?

With the wings across half the crop's width, as the reach calculator frames it: with 600 mm on a 24-megapixel full-frame body, up to about 140 m for a 2,048-pixel-wide image and 80 m for an A4 print at 300 ppi; on the Canon EOS R7, 270 m and 150 m.
