Canon EOS R6 Mark II for bird photography: the numbers, worked out

What the Canon EOS R6 Mark II puts on a bird, worked out from its sensor and the lenses people pair with it: pixels across birds from chickadee to heron at 10, 25 and 50 m, how far you can crop and still meet iNaturalist and contest sizes, and how much the wingtips blur in flight.

Short answer: the Canon EOS R6 Mark II records 6,000 × 4,000 pixels (24.0 megapixels) on a full-frame sensor of 35.9 × 23.9 mm, so each pixel is 6.0 µm across. With a 500 mm lens that puts about 819 pixels across an American robin at 25 m: crop to iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels and the robin still fills 40% of the width. Crop from the full-size original, and never upscale a crop to reach a size.

Does the 1.6x crop mode add reach?

No. In 1.6x crop mode the R6 Mark II keeps the middle of the frame, about 3,750 × 2,500 pixels (9.4 megapixels) instead of 6,000 × 4,000, but the bird gets exactly the same pixels: 819 across an American robin at 25 m with 500 mm either way. The bird looks bigger in the viewfinder because the frame around it is smaller (22% of the width instead of 14%). Shooting the full frame and cropping later keeps every pixel of the bird and leaves room to straighten the horizon or include a wingtip you'd otherwise cut off.

Crop tighter and the file drops below the size; enlarging it to reach the number adds no detail, and the contests that ask for full-size files say so (crop rules and minimum sizes for 11 contests). Holding the osprey's wingtips within 3 pixels at this distance would take about 1/12800; for the bald eagle, about 1/10000. The wings slow to a stop at the top and bottom of each stroke, which is why a burst catches sharp wingtips that a single frame usually misses.

Questions

How many pixels does the Canon EOS R6 Mark II put on a bird?

It depends on the lens and the distance. With 500 mm, about 819 pixels across an American robin at 25 m and 405 at 50 m; with 800 mm, 1,326 and 652. The sum: the bird's length × focal length ÷ (distance − focal length), divided by the pixel pitch (6.0 µm).

How far can you crop an R6 Mark II photo for iNaturalist or a contest?

A horizontal crop has to keep 34% of the frame's width to fill iNaturalist's 2,048 pixels, 50% for Audubon's 3,000-pixel entries and 58% for Australian Geographic's 3,500-pixel finalist files (2026 rules). Crop from the full-size original and never enlarge a crop to reach a size.

Does the R6 Mark II's 1.6x crop mode give more reach?

No. It keeps about 3,750 × 2,500 pixels from the middle of the frame instead of 6,000 × 4,000, but the bird gets the same pixels: 819 across an American robin at 25 m with a 500 mm lens either way. Shooting the full frame and cropping later keeps the same bird with more room around it.

What shutter speed freezes a bird in flight with the R6 Mark II?

With an osprey at about 36 m on 500 mm, wings across two thirds of the frame, the wingtips blur about 17 pixels at 1/2000 and 9 at 1/4000 mid-stroke when you pan with the bird. Holding them within 3 pixels would take about 1/12800, so a burst is what catches the wings sharp near the top or bottom of the stroke. Wingbeat rates for the osprey and bald eagle are estimates.
