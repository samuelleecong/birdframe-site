Crop sensor or full frame for bird photography?

With the same lens at the same distance, the bird's image on the sensor is the same size whatever the camera, so a crop sensor puts more pixels on the bird only if it packs its pixels more densely. A Canon EOS R7 (APS-C) puts 1.37 times as many pixels across the bird as a full-frame Canon EOS R5. A Nikon Z50II puts only 1.03 times as many as a Nikon Z8, and a Sony A6700 the same as a Sony A7R V. The crop mode on a full-frame camera adds no pixels at all.

Short answer: when you're short of focal length, as you usually are with small birds, pixel density decides how much detail lands on the bird, not sensor size. Compare pixels per millimetre of sensor width: 312 on the R7, 228 on the R5. Full frame keeps real advantages: a wider view at the same focal length, which helps with birds in flight, and cleaner files in low light when the bird fills the frame. Whichever you use, crop from the full-size original, because every pixel on the bird counts.

Same lens, same image, different pixels

A lens projects the bird onto the sensor at a size set by the focal length and the distance, not by the camera behind it. A 24 cm American Robin at 20 m (66 ft) with a 600 mm lens is 7.42 mm long on any sensor. A smaller sensor takes in less of the scene around the bird, so the bird looks bigger in the frame. Whether it holds more detail depends on how many pixels cover those 7.42 mm.

Pixels across the bird = image size on the sensor × pixels across the sensor ÷ sensor width. A Canon EOS R7 has 6,960 pixels across 22.3 mm, 312 per millimetre, so the robin's 7.42 mm cover about 2,317 pixels. A Canon EOS R5 has 8,192 across 36 mm, 228 per millimetre: about 1,689 pixels.

Megapixels are the size of the image each camera saves, which can be a little under the maker's headline figure: the A7R V, sold as a 61-megapixel camera, saves 9,504 × 6,336 pixels (60.2 MP). Open each pair side by side in the calculator: R7 and R5, 2,317 and 1,689 pixels; Z50II and Z8 in DX mode, 1,759 and 1,707 pixels; A6700 and A7R V, 1,973 and 1,976 pixels; OM-1 and A1, 2,211 and 1,786 pixels.

  • Canon EOS R7 vs EOS R5: the R7 wins by 1.37 times, because its 32 MP sit on a sensor 22.3 mm wide: 312 pixels per millimetre against 228. This is the case people mean when they say a crop sensor gives more reach.
  • Nikon Z50II vs Z8: nearly a tie, 1.03 times. The Z50II's 21 MP on APS-C are only a little denser than the Z8's 45 MP on full frame, 237 pixels per millimetre against 230. In DX mode the Z8 saves a 5,504 × 3,669 pixel picture (20 MP) with the same 1,707 pixels across the bird: almost exactly what the Z50II gives.
  • Sony A6700 vs A7R V: a tie. Both pack about 266 pixels into each millimetre, so the bird gets 1,973 and 1,976 pixels. The A7R V's APS-C mode saves a 6,336 × 4,224 picture (27 MP), a little more than the A6700's whole 26 MP frame.
  • OM System OM-1 vs Sony A1: the OM-1 wins by 1.24 times, 298 pixels per millimetre against 241, with 20 MP to the A1's 50 MP. The A1 shows far more of the scene around the bird, which matters for birds in flight and for leaving room to frame the crop.

Crop mode adds no pixels

An APS-C or DX crop mode on a full-frame camera records only the middle of the sensor. The bird gets exactly the same pixels as before; the picture just loses its edges, as if you'd cropped it later. On a Canon EOS R5 the 1.6× crop mode turns the 8,192 × 5,464 frame into 5,120 × 3,415 (17 MP), and the robin above gets 1,689 pixels either way (R5 in crop mode, 1,689 pixels). Crop mode can still help in the field, with a bigger view of the bird in the viewfinder and smaller files. But it drops the edges for good, so a bird that drifts towards the side of the frame can end up cut off.

What full frame still does better

  • A wider view. At 600 mm and 20 m, an R5 takes in a scene about 1.16 m (3.8 ft) wide and an R7 about 0.72 m (2.4 ft). The wider view makes a bird in flight easier to find and keep in the frame, and leaves room to choose the crop later.
  • Cleaner files in low light, when you can fill the frame. If you can get close enough, or use a long enough lens, to frame the bird the same way, the bigger sensor gathers more light for that picture.
  • Density and width together. A high-resolution full-frame body has APS-C-like density and full-frame width: the A7R V matches the A6700 on the bird and still has the whole wider picture around it.

The crop sensor's edge shows when you're short of focal length: the bird is small in the frame even at your longest lens, which is most small-bird photography. There, a dense APS-C or Micro Four Thirds body works like a longer lens on full frame: a Canon EOS R7 at 400 mm puts about as many pixels on the bird as an R5 at 550 mm (1,529 and 1,544 pixels). Questions

Does a crop sensor give you more reach?

It doesn't magnify the bird: the lens projects the same-size image on any sensor. It puts more pixels on the bird only if its pixels are denser. A Canon EOS R7 puts 1.37 times as many pixels across the bird as a Canon EOS R5 with the same lens, but a Nikon Z50II only 1.03 times as many as a Nikon Z8.

Does APS-C crop mode give more reach?

No. It records the middle of the sensor and drops the rest, so the bird gets exactly the same pixels; it only looks bigger in a smaller picture. Cropping the full photo afterwards gives the same result and keeps your options open.

Is a 45 MP full-frame camera as good as APS-C for birds?

Close to some APS-C bodies: with 600 mm at 20 m, a Nikon Z8 puts 1,707 pixels across a 24 cm American Robin and a Nikon Z50II 1,759. A Canon EOS R7 puts 2,317, because its pixels are denser still.

Is Micro Four Thirds good for bird photography?

For pixels on the bird, yes. An OM System OM-1 packs 298 pixels into each millimetre of sensor, more than any full-frame camera on this page, so it puts 1.24 times as many pixels on the bird as a Sony A1 with the same lens. Its smaller sensor gathers less light for the same framing, so low light is where full frame pulls ahead.
